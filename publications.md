#### Job Market Paper

**From the Ivory Tower to Capitol Hill: Gender, Mobility, and Expert Advisory Work**
*with Yotam Sofer*

Examining academic economists' engagement with public policymakers, this paper investigates whether geographic mobility friction contributes to gender disparities in expert testimony before the U.S. Congress. We argue that expert engagement is a spatially concentrated activity and that women face systematically higher costs of geographic mobility, constituting an overlooked supply-side constraint on their participation in high-impact expert advisory work. Using a COVID-19-related policy shift from in-person to online hearings as a source of exogenous variation in mobility requirements, we show that reducing geographic friction significantly changed the composition of expert witnesses. Constructing a dataset of the universe of economists who have testified since 1969 and a large at-risk group of intellectually proximate economists, we find a consistent female disadvantage in the odds of appearing as a witness. Geographic distance from Washington, DC reduces the odds of testifying for all economists, but has a stronger effect on women. Following the shift to online hearings, female economists were significantly more likely to testify relative to men. Working-from-anywhere policies, by lowering mobility friction, can broaden organizations' access to a wider pool of experts.

#### Working Papers

**Do Women Ask for Less? The Gender Gap in Science Funding**
*with Lorenzo Palladini, Hans Christian Kongsted, Valentina Tartari, and Kris Gulati — under review, Nature*

Research funding shapes which scientific questions get pursued and at what scale, making access to grants one of the most consequential forces in a research career. Women receive fewer and smaller grants than men across many fields, an inequality that may compound over time and steer science away from the questions women are more likely to ask. Most research has examined gender gaps in grant success rates, but the amount a researcher ultimately receives depends not only on their probability of being awarded funding, but also on how much they request in the first place — a stage that has rarely been observed directly. Here we show, using application-level records from two large European philanthropic science funders, that women request substantially less funding than comparable men. Controlling for applicant track record, proposal quality as assessed by external reviewers, and fixed effects for funder, grant type, university, and subfield, we find that women request 5.4 per cent less at the proposal stage — a gap that accounts for roughly three-quarters of the well-known gap in funding awarded. Making a smaller request does not improve the odds of success, so the gap does not reflect a winning strategy. Instead, we find that the gap is largest among more junior and less experienced researchers and narrows as researchers apply repeatedly, pointing to differences in experience rather than project scope. Despite receiving less, women produce as much research from each grant as men, yet their smaller awards result in the formation of smaller teams that constrain the broader output sustaining scientific careers. These findings relocate a central source of inequality in science from how proposals are judged to how they are constructed.

#### Work in Progress

**Gender Disparities in Academic Expertise: Implications for the Direction of Public Policy**
*Data collection phase*

Extant literature links scientists’ gender to the direction of science and innovation, which prompts the question: What happens when women are underrepresented amongst the experts that advise influential decision-makers? In this paper, I posit that descriptive representation of women amongst expert advisors shapes which problems receive attention and whose interests are served in the solutions that are suggested. Using an exogenous policy change that increased women's participation as expert witnesses during the COVID-19 pandemic in congressional hearings I plan to investigate how gender representation changes the dynamics and substantive content of high-level decision-makers’ deliberations.
